# Calibration notes: official sample papers

These notes describe the three official practice papers (Practice Tests 1–3, each with Reading, Thinking Skills and Mathematical Reasoning) so that new tests can be calibrated **without the papers attached**. They are written entirely in our own words: no passages, questions or option sets are reproduced. The papers themselves are copyright (NSW Department of Education / Cambridge) and must never be committed.

Sources used: all nine question papers and all nine official "explained answers" documents (Reading, Thinking Skills and Maths for PT1–PT3).

Referencing convention used in `content/vNN.json` `sample_map`: `PT2 Q17` = Practice Test 2, question 17 of that section.

When the papers *are* attached, still prefer them for deep recalibration; these notes are the everyday reference.

---

## 1. The real test at a glance

| Section | Questions | Time | Options | Pace |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reading | 33 (Q1–33) | 40 min | A–D (missing sentences: a bank lettered A–G) | ~70 s per question incl. reading |
| Thinking Skills | 30 | 30 min | A–D | 60 s per question |
| Mathematical Reasoning | 35 | 40 min | A–E | ~70 s per question |

- No penalty for wrong answers; separate answer sheet; no calculators.
- Difficulty is not strictly increasing, but the last third of each paper is clearly harder (more steps, more constraints, subtler traps). "Stretch" in our tests = the level of roughly Q25–35 (Maths), Q20–30 (Thinking), and the inference questions at the end of each Reading task.

## 2. Reading

Every paper uses the same five tasks in the same order:

| Task | Questions | Format | Our equivalent |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Q1–6 | Story extract + 6 multiple-choice | `story` |
| 2 | Q7–14 | Cloze passage, 8 gaps, 4 single-word options each | `cloze` |
| 3 | Q15–19 | Poem + 5 multiple-choice | `poem` |
| 4 | Q20–25 | Missing sentences: 6 gaps, a bank of 7 sentences (A–G), one not used | `missing sentences` |
| 5 | Q26–33 | Four short extracts on one theme, 8 "which extract…" questions | `four extracts` |

### 2.1 Passages: length, source, level

- **Story extracts** are long (roughly 700–1,000 words), from real published fiction, both contemporary and classic (one is an Edwardian children's classic). Topics: a girl in a rural African village gathering firewood; boys daring each other at a "haunted" river; children who hatch a magical bird. Settings are vivid, vocabulary is rich (*reverently*, *disconsolate*-level words appear naturally), and there is always a layer of feeling or humour under the action.
- **Cloze passages** are first-person or journalistic non-fiction, ~200–250 words: a first-time author seeing his book in a shop, a record-breaking topic, a keen birdwatcher.
- **Poems** are by real poets (Australian modern and 19th-century classic), 12–30 short lines, often free verse; one is rhymed and stanza-numbered. Themes: close observation of a cat; looking at tiny things until they become a world; a storm seen through a cottage window.
- **Missing-sentence texts** are magazine-style natural history, science or history of technology, ~450–550 words, written with personality (asides, humour, comparisons to the reader). Topics: a bird that drums with sticks; an Australian bird often mistaken for an owl; the history of escalators.
- **Four extracts** share a theme (the sea; water; colour) and mix genres: memoir, fiction, factual report, opinion. Each extract is ~120–200 words.

**Implication for us:** our 250–350-word passages are shorter than the real thing (fine for a 3-question format), but vocabulary and sentence complexity should match the samples, and every few tests should use a real public-domain classic.

### 2.2 Question types (from all three papers and the explanations)

Story and poem questions, roughly in order of frequency:

1. **What a detail shows / why the writer includes it** ("When the writer mentions X, she is making the point that…"). Most common. The correct answer is an *implication*, not a restatement.
2. **Feelings or attitudes shown rather than stated** (how a character feels at a moment; how others now regard him; how she treats a special object).
3. **Meaning of a word or phrase in context** (e.g. what a single adverb tells us about the character's attitude).
4. **What happens / literal check** (what the cat carries away; what a character does at a place). Present but rarer, usually early in a task.
5. **Whole-text questions, always last in the task:** the writer's technique and its effect (humour that makes a story less menacing; mixed formal/informal language of a character); the overall impression of a character; the viewpoint from which a scene is seen; what the title prepares us for.
6. **Poem-specific:** what is compared with what; which thing is given human feelings; the purpose of repetition or of the one true rhyme; what a line or group of lines is doing (describing how others see the speaker); what "it" refers to.

**Trap patterns** (from the explained answers): the best wrong option is usually
- a real detail from the text attached to the wrong question (the character *did* boast, but about something else);
- true but too strong or too literal (taking a comparison at face value);
- the opposite emotion of the true one;
- something the text explicitly rules out ("there is no mention of how to fight him").

### 2.3 Cloze

- 8 gaps; each set of 4 options is the **same part of speech** and close in meaning.
- What decides the answer, in order of frequency:
  1. **Fixed expressions and collocations**: *contain oneself*, *the culmination of years of work*, *twists and turns*, *springs to mind*, *teeming with*, *stand (a record)*, *set (a record)*.
  2. **Grammar/preposition fit**: only one verb takes the following preposition or object.
  3. **Shade of meaning**: near-synonyms where one is wrong for the context (*leafed* vs *paged* through a book; *decline* vs *decay* of bird populations).
- Vocabulary level runs from upper primary to adult: *culmination, pinnacle, scouring, teeming, remiss, proportion, triumphantly, summoned*. Easy everyday idioms are **not** typical.

### 2.4 Missing sentences

- 6 gaps, one sentence in the bank is never used. Gaps are spread through all paragraphs.
- The deciding clues (named in the official explanations):
  - **Reference words** pointing back: *This*, *here*, *in the area*, *they*, *The birds*, *it*.
  - **General statement → example** ("individual males differed…" followed by "One of them…").
  - **Contrast and correction** ("Not that we blame anyone for the mistake").
  - **Elaboration** of the sentence before (adds a reason, a comparison to humans, a consequence).
- The unused sentence is on-topic and plausible; it fails on reference or order, not on subject.

### 2.5 Four extracts

- 8 questions, each answer is one extract; every extract is the answer to **at least one** question, usually two.
- Question wording ranges from near-paraphrase ("refers to a dangerous substance") to inference ("suggests newcomers are not always welcome"; "reveals the writer was out of place"; "explains a positive effect on one's mental outlook"; "uses figurative language"; "mentions words of wisdom that helped").
- Harder items need you to recognise an idea expressed in completely different words, or to spot a technique (figurative language) rather than a topic.

### 3.2 How the critical-thinking items are built

- **Spot the mistake**: a box of facts, a character's conclusion, four options. Every option is a "may" sentence. The right one names the specific gap in the reasoning. Wrong options are true-sounding but irrelevant (cost, other people's preferences, a different benefit) or even support the speaker. Mistake patterns seen: ignoring another cause; judging from your own small group or experience; needed ≠ guaranteed; unfair comparison (totals vs rates, different opponents); "most" ≠ "all"; reversed rule; assuming only two possibilities; assuming one cause rules out another; one matching feature taken as proof.
- **Supports the claim**: a recommendation with a goal ("do X so that Y"). The answer supplies the missing link X → Y. Traps: a true fact about X that doesn't reach Y; a correlation (people who do Y also like X); a general statement about Y that doesn't involve X; something that works against the claim.
- **Whose reasoning**: a box with a rule, two named speakers each drawing a conclusion, options "A only / B only / both / neither". Across the papers the answers are spread over all four (e.g. first speaker only, second only, both, neither all occur), so never let one answer become predictable. Patterns: converse (reversed rule), needed vs guaranteed, "most" → "probably" (valid one way only), worst-case certainty (how many to draw to be sure), "only if" rules where both conclusions hold.
- **Must / cannot be true**: from a strict rule, pick the only safe conclusion (or the impossible one).

### 3.3 Non-verbal and data items

- **Spatial** (one or two clear steps, never elaborate): side/top views of block models; nets of dice (opposite faces); identical vs mirrored shapes; tiles rotated not flipped; pieces that pair into layers or squares; folding string/paper; viewpoints around a table; compass directions on a grid.
- **Tables and timetables**: small tables (4–10 rows) with one twist: a cancelled day, a best-of-two rule, a swap, a total versus a count of wins, a lunch break.
- **Order/arrangement**: 3–6 items in a line, around a table or in a car; 3–4 clues; usually one clue that only works after another is placed.

### 3.4 Difficulty climb

- Q1–10: one rule or one table, one step.
- Q11–20: two constraints, or a critical-thinking item with a tempting near-miss.
- Q21–30: multiple constraints (truth-tellers and liars, a missing fourth clue, voting combinations), whose-reasoning items where both or neither are right, spatial items needing two mental moves.

### 4.2 Typical distractors (from the explained answers and option sets)

- Truncating instead of rounding; rounding 5 down.
- Forgetting one step of a multi-step problem (e.g. forgetting the minutes when converting time; forgetting the hours across midnight).
- Using the number that appears in the question instead of the result (the total instead of the difference; the bought amount instead of what is left).
- Off-by-one: fence posts, counting inclusive days, dates across month ends.
- Halving or doubling at the wrong point; using the wrong whole for a fraction.
- Adding numerators and denominators.
- Reading every small mark on a scale as 1 unit.
- Counting the overlap twice (two-way tables, shared walls/edges, "both" categories).

### 4.3 Difficulty climb

- Q1–10: one or two steps, everyday contexts (money, time, scales, place value).
- Q11–25: two to three steps, often a diagram or graph, sometimes a "which statements are correct" format.
- Q26–35: several steps or one key insight (working backwards, product codes, combined rates, folding/cutting, route counting). Usually one competition-style item near the end.

## 5. Using these notes when building a test

- **Pick the skill first**, from the maps above, then write a fresh question in a new context. Never re-create a sample question with new numbers.
- **Stretch questions** (`stretch` in `vNN.json`): model them on the Q21–30 (Thinking) / Q26–35 (Maths) items and the final whole-text or inference items of each Reading task.
- **Distractors**: take them from the trap lists in 2.2, 3.2 and 4.2.
- **`sample_map`**: cite the closest one or two items using the tables above (e.g. "PT3 Q32 (two people working together)").
